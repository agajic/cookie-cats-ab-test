What a gate actually does

Cookie Cats is a mobile puzzle game. At level 30 it stops you. To carry on you wait out a timer, or you pay to skip it, or you pester a friend. That wall is a gate, and it quietly does two jobs at once: it paces the game, and it sells.

The team wanted to know what happens if the wall moves later, to level 40. Players would get ten more levels of free play before hitting it. Would more of them come back?

So they ran a randomised experiment. Every new player was assigned at install to one of two versions, and then simply observed: did they come back the next day, and did they come back a week later?

My prediction, on the record

Before I computed a single retention rate I wrote down what I expected to find. That small discipline has a large payoff: it stops you quietly rewriting the question once you have seen the answer. Statisticians call the formal version pre-registration; it is the main defence against fooling yourself.

Theory A: momentum

A player who reaches level 40 has invested more and formed more of a habit, so they are more likely to push through the wall and come back. A later gate should retain better.

What I predicted

Theory B: the forced break

A gate is an enforced pause. Hitting it earlier stops players while they still want more, so they come back to finish. Let them play to exhaustion and they simply stop. An earlier gate should retain better.

What the data supported

I backed Theory A. I was wrong, and being wrong is the reason this project is worth reading: the result is not obvious, so it had to be measured rather than argued about in a meeting.

I also fixed my primary metric in advance: 7-day retention, not 1-day. Nearly every game has decent day-one retention. That is novelty, and it is noisy. Day seven is the first honest sign that a habit formed. Choosing before looking is what stops you from later picking whichever metric happened to be significant.

Trusting the data before trusting the result

An experiment can only claim cause if the two groups are otherwise identical. Three checks came before any comparison of retention.

Was the coin fair?

Assignment should split roughly 50/50. It came out 44,700 against 45,489, or 49.56% versus 50.44%. A binomial test flags that as unlikely for a fair coin: p = 0.0087. But look at the scale before panicking. Across 90,189 players one standard deviation of random wobble is only ±0.17 percentage points, so a 0.44-point deviation sits about 2.6 standard deviations out. It is detectable precisely because the sample is enormous.

So it is statistically detectable and practically trivial. Decisive point: the gate cannot have caused it. Players are assigned when they install, long before anyone reaches level 30 or 40, so the treatment has no mechanism to influence who lands where. I proceeded, and wrote the caveat down rather than burying it.

One player who was not a player

The engagement column tops out at 49,854 rounds in 14 days, about 3,561 a day. That is not a human; it is a bot or a logging error. The next-highest player managed 2,961. I dropped that row.

It later turned out to make no difference at all to the retention result: the gap moved from 0.820 to 0.818 points. A single row cannot shift a proportion measured across 90,000 people. That is a useful thing to know rather than assume: an outlier's danger depends entirely on the metric. It would have wrecked an average, or a model using round counts, while a rate barely notices it.

Most players barely play

The engagement distribution is brutally skewed. 4.4% of players install and never really start, and the median player finishes just 16 rounds. A large share of any game's audience is decided before the design gets a chance.

The result

With the data checked, the comparison is simple arithmetic, and the answer is uncomfortably small.

Moving the gate to level 40 cost 0.82 percentage points of 7-day retention, a 4.3% relative decline. Written down, that looks like nothing. The entire remaining question is whether a gap that small is a real signal or just the coin wobbling.

Is it real, or is it noise?

Retention is a yes/no outcome per player, so this is a comparison of two proportions. I used a chi-square test of independence. It asks whether coming back is linked to which group you were in, or independent of it — and ran a two-proportion z-test alongside as a cross-check. For a 2×2 table they are the same mathematics, so agreement is a sanity check rather than new evidence.

In plain words: if the two gates truly retained identically, a gap at least this large would appear by chance about once in every 600 experiments. "It was chance" is a poor explanation for a one-in-600 event, so the difference is real.

The trap here is the one most people fall into. A 0.82-point gap looks small to a human eye, but "small to a human" was never the question. "Bigger than the noise" is. And at this sample size the noise is tiny.

Being significant is not the same as being important, though. Significance answers is it real. It says nothing about how big or how sure, which is what anyone deciding the game's design actually needs.

A thousand versions of the same experiment

A p-value hands you a verdict. It does not hand you a range. So I resampled the players with replacement a thousand times, recomputing the retention gap in each simulated world, and looked at the spread of answers.

1,000 out of 1,000

In not one of a thousand redrawn worlds did gate 40 come out ahead. The 95% interval, +0.32 to +1.34 points, never crosses zero, so even the most pessimistic plausible world still has the earlier gate winning.

That is the sentence worth handing a product manager, and it is why a bootstrap earns its place on top of the test: not "significant or not", but a small effect with a direction that is not in doubt.

The second opinion

Day-7 retention was my primary metric, chosen in advance. Day-1 retention is the secondary check: does the shorter horizon tell the same story?

It points the same way (gate 30 ahead by 0.59 points) but does not reach significance (p = 0.075). That is not a contradiction, and it is worth being precise about why: day-1 retention is noisier and easier to move with novelty, which is exactly the argument I used to make day 7 primary before running anything. Consistent direction across both horizons, with the cleaner signal on the metric I had already committed to.

The recommendation

Keep the gate at level 30. Do not ship the move to 40.

Moving it cost 0.8 points of 7-day retention, about 4% of the players who would otherwise have formed a habit. Small per player, but retention compounds into day 30, day 90 and lifetime value, and across millions of installs it is a great many people.

How confident, and in what: very confident in the direction; the size is a range, +0.3 to +1.3 points; and the reason, a forced-break effect, is a plausible explanation this test did not prove. Direction, magnitude and mechanism deserve different amounts of confidence, and saying so is not hedging.

The model that looked too good

Beyond the experiment, can we predict which players come back at all? I fitted a logistic regression (binary outcome, readable coefficients, the simplest thing that could work) using early engagement, day-1 return, and group.

It scored ROC-AUC 0.881. I used AUC rather than accuracy deliberately: 81% of players never return, so a model that lazily predicts "won't return" every time already scores 81% accuracy while being worthless. AUC measures ranking, and imbalance cannot flatter it.

Then the number that should make you suspicious. 0.88 is very good. Too good for three crude features. So I checked what the strongest one actually contains.

sum_gamerounds counts rounds played over the first 14 days. The thing being predicted is whether a player returned on day 7. The feature covers a window that runs past the event it is predicting, so it partly contains the answer.

A player with 200 rounds over a fortnight was obviously still around on day seven. That is data leakage, and it is one of the most common ways a model looks brilliant in testing and collapses in production. So I removed the leaking feature and refitted using only signals genuinely available before day 7.

0.716 is the honest number, and the one I would report. Day-1 return legitimately predicts day-7 return because day one genuinely comes first. The lesson outlives the score: always ask whether a feature would really be available, and would not already encode the answer, at the moment you would have to make the prediction.

Worth noting too: the gate group barely predicts an individual's retention (−0.04). The gate matters in aggregate across 90,000 players and is nearly noise for any one of them. That is not a contradiction; it is what a small average effect looks like up close.

What I would check next

  • Revenue, not just retention. The gate is also where players pay to skip. Gate 40 could earn more per player even while retaining fewer. This dataset has no revenue column, so that trade-off is genuinely unmeasured here. A full decision needs it.
  • Segment casual against heavy players. An average can hide two opposite effects cancelling out.
  • A longer window. Seven days cannot show whether the effect fades or compounds by day 30 or 90.
  • A formal power analysis, to state the smallest effect this design could reliably detect rather than assuming the sample was ample.
